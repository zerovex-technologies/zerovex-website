3-Axis vs 4-Axis vs 5-Axis CNC Programming: Which to Choose?

Published 14 October 2026 · ZEROVEX Technologies, Coimbatore

When a part comes in for programming, one of the first questions is how many axes it needs. The most advanced option is not always the right one. The right choice depends on the part, the machine you have, and how much setup time you want to remove.

3-axis programming: the default for most milling

In 3-axis machining the tool moves along X, Y and Z while the part stays fixed in the setup. It is simple to fixture, quick to program and economical to run, which is why it covers most plates, brackets, covers, fixtures and many mould inserts. It handles pockets, profiles, holes and 3D surfaces. See our 3-axis CAM programming page.

The limit is access. Features on other faces need another setup, deep features may need long tools, and undercuts cannot be reached.

4-axis programming: adding a rotary axis

A fourth axis adds rotation, usually about the X axis on a vertical machine. It can be used in two ways:

  • Indexed 4-axis: the part rotates to a set angle and a normal 3-axis operation is cut. Good for parts with features on several sides.
  • Continuous 4-axis: the rotary axis moves while cutting, for wrapped or helical features around a cylinder.

This brings several sides of a part into one setup, which reduces re-clamping and keeps features aligned to each other. More on 4-axis CAM programming.

5-axis programming: 3+2 and simultaneous

Five-axis adds two rotary axes. There are two ways to use them:

  • 3+2 (positional): the rotary axes tilt the part and lock, then a 3-axis cut is run. This gives access to angled faces in one setup.
  • Simultaneous: all five axes move together during the cut, so the tool can follow complex surfaces continuously.

Five-axis can reduce setups and allow shorter, stiffer tools, but it asks more of the whole process: the machine's kinematics, the post processor, and careful collision checking. Details on 5-axis simultaneous programming.

How to decide

  • How many faces of the part need machining, and can they be done in one clamping?
  • Are there cylindrical, wrapped or helical features? That points to 4-axis.
  • Are there undercuts, deep pockets or complex curved surfaces? That points to 5-axis.
  • What does your machine have: a plain VMC, a VMC with a rotary unit, an HMC with an indexing table, or a 5-axis machine?
  • How many parts are needed? A few extra programming hours can pay back over a larger batch.

Do not forget the post processor

Rotary-axis output depends heavily on the post processor. A post that does not match your machine configuration and controller can produce wrong motion, so 4-axis and 5-axis work often needs a post that is edited or built for the machine. See post processor customization.
